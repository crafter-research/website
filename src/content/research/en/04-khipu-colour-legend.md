## The question

Khipus are the knotted cords the Inka state used to keep records. Since Leland Locke's work in 1912 we know how to read most of their numbers: knot type and position give a base-10 value. What nobody can read is the rest. A cord also has a colour, a twist, an attachment side and a place in the hierarchy. Spanish chroniclers wrote that specialists read histories and laws from these devices, and no translated khipu has survived.

We started from one narrow, testable question: does the colour of a cord predict the number knotted on it?

## Data

- The [Open Khipu Repository](https://github.com/khipulab/open-khipu-repository) (OKR) v2.1.0: 619 khipus, 54,403 cords and 110,677 knots. We reproduced those counts before doing anything else.
- The [Khipu Field Guide](https://www.khipufieldguide.com) workbooks for 611 of those khipus.
- For the Santa Valley case: Medrano and Urton (2018), FitzPatrick (2024), and Urton's *Inka History in Knots* (2017), chapter 12 and its appendix with all 133 six-cord groups.

## How we worked

Every test followed the same protocol:

1. Write the belief as one falsifiable sentence, with its oracle and what would kill it.
2. Commit that preregistration before computing any outcome.
3. Run against a null: the same model with colours shuffled, so any gain has to come from the real colours.
4. Report the verdict in the same place whether the belief survives or dies.
5. Hand the code and the data, not our conclusions, to an independent reviewer.

The reviewer was Fable, run separately with read-only access. It reviewed the first card and then the whole trajectory. Both reviews changed our verdicts.

## What we tested

| Test | Belief | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| K1 | Cord attributes predict values on khipus from unseen sites | Killed: worse than plain digit frequencies |
| K1, K2b, K5 | Within a khipu, colour predicts value size beyond its cluster | Survived three times, then withdrawn (see K7) |
| K2a | The colour code is shared by khipus from the same site | Untested: the models did worse than guessing |
| K3 | The Santa Valley first cords add up to the 1670 tribute | Reproduced in structure, not in total (see below) |
| K4 | Values at a fixed position of repeating units rise like running totals | Killed, and the test measured the wrong thing |
| K6 | A cord that is the sum of others has its own colour | Retracted: an artefact of colour bands |
| K7 | The colour signal survives a layout control, in both recording traditions | Failed our minimum effect size in both |

The full record, including the dead ends, is in the [repository](https://github.com/crafter-research/khipu).

## The result that decided it

K5 looked strong: on held-out clusters, the real colours beat all 200 shuffles with a calibrated model. The review pointed out that colour and position are tangled. Cords in the same position of repeating groups tend to share both a colour and a magnitude, so part of the "colour" signal was really layout.

K7 added a layout control and ran the test separately on khipus recorded in the Ascher tradition (56) and the Urton tradition (202). We fixed three conditions before running: beat the shuffles, save at least 0.02 bits per cord, and be positive in more than half of the khipus.

Both traditions beat the shuffles, and most khipus are positive: 75% of the Ascher khipus and 58% of the Urton khipus. But the gain is 0.016 bits per cord in both, about 1.5% of what the model spends per cord. That is below our minimum, so by our own rule the colour legend claim is withdrawn.

What we can still say: colour carries a small and consistent amount of khipu-local information about the size of a cord's number, beyond position, and it is not a habit of one research team. It is too small to call a legend.

## The Santa Valley case

Six khipus from the Santa Valley have been matched to a 1670 census of San Pedro de Corongo: 132 tributaries in six lineages, owing 367 pesos. It is the closest thing to a Rosetta stone the field has.

- The structure reproduces. The recto and verso attachment of the first cords matches the published counts within one cord.
- Urton's appendix gives first-cord sums of 48, 80, 101, 84, 36 and 10: a total of 359. Adding the values he imputes to two broken cords gives the 367. Twelve other first cords are zero and stay at zero. The match is real, but its exactness depends on that choice.
- FitzPatrick (2024) cites moiety totals of 381 or 382 and 86. Those do not add up to 367. If the recto total is 86, the verso total must be 281 or 282, so 381 may be a misprint. We have not checked the English edition, so we do not claim it.

## A data issue

On one Santa khipu (KH0326), OKR records single knots in the tens place as hundreds: 15 becomes 105. After moving that digit, 106 of its 108 pendants match the Khipu Field Guide, and Urton's appendix agrees with the corrected values. The pattern also shows up on two other khipus, KH0472 and KH0401, but there we have no source outside the OKR lineage. We will share this with the OKR maintainers.

## What we got wrong

- K6 survived only because our null shuffled colours across the whole khipu, which breaks up the colour bands. A null that keeps the bands intact killed it.
- K6 also had a parsing bug that dropped half the relations. We saw the buggy verdict before fixing it, and we kept that run in the record.
- We treated the Khipu Field Guide as an independent check on OKR. Both come from the same Harvard database, so their 96.7% agreement is shared lineage, not verification.
- Our first "recorder" control used the person who typed the data, not the person who recorded the khipu.
- Our preregistrations were committed locally. From this note on, they are public before analysis.

## What stands

1. OKR codes tens as hundreds on KH0326 (high confidence, three sources agree).
2. Urton's 367 pesos is a measured 359 plus two imputed broken cords (high confidence).
3. Colour predicts value size inside a khipu by about 0.016 bits per cord beyond layout, in both traditions (real, but below the size we set).
4. Whether 381 is a misprint for 281 (open).

## Next

The most valuable missing piece is not a model. Cord spacing is almost never recorded: 0.008% of pendants in OKR. Tests about units, running totals and grouping need it, and it has to come from photographs. That is our next instrument.
